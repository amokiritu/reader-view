The Quiet Return of the Night Train

By Maya Okafor · November 2, 2026

ShareTweetEmail

For most of the last forty years, the overnight sleeper train looked like a relic. Budget airlines could move a traveler across a continent in two hours for the price of a sandwich, and rail operators, squeezed by maintenance costs and empty berths, quietly retired route after route. Yet over the past several seasons, something unexpected has happened: the night train has started to come back.

New services now link capitals that had not been connected by rail after dark in a generation. Carriages that once sat rusting in sidings have been refurbished with private compartments, proper mattresses, and breakfast delivered to the door. Operators report that many departures sell out weeks in advance, particularly on Friday evenings and during school holidays.

Why travelers are choosing to sleep on the rails

Part of the appeal is arithmetic. A traveler who boards at nine in the evening and wakes up in another city at seven saves the cost of a hotel night and skips the trip out to a distant airport. Part of it is environmental: a seat on an electric train produces a small fraction of the emissions of a short-haul flight, a difference many passengers now weigh when they book.

But the people who ride these trains often describe something harder to measure. There is the slow pleasure of watching the lights of towns slide past the window, of falling asleep to the rhythm of the wheels, and of stepping onto a platform in the middle of a city rather than on its outskirts.

“You don't lose a day to travel,” one regular passenger said. “You gain an evening.”

The obstacles that remain

The revival is fragile. Cross-border routes have to satisfy several different rail authorities, each with its own safety rules, signaling systems, and track access fees. Rolling stock is scarce, and new sleeper carriages take years to design and build. Some services that launched with fanfare have already been cut back after struggling to fill their cabins midweek.

  • Track access charges can make up a large share of a ticket's cost.
  • Booking systems are often fragmented across national operators.
  • Night maintenance windows sometimes force long detours.

Even so, planners are cautiously optimistic. Several governments have begun to treat overnight rail as a piece of public infrastructure rather than a commercial afterthought, offering subsidies and guaranteed track slots. If those commitments hold, the next few years could decide whether the night train becomes a permanent part of how people travel, or remains a charming niche.

For now, the overnight departures keep leaving on schedule, their windows glowing as they pull away into the dark.
