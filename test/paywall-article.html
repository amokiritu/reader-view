City Libraries Are Quietly Becoming Repair Shops

From toasters to tablets, branches are lending tools and expertise to keep everyday objects out of landfills.

By Jordan Reyes

Walk into the central branch on a Saturday morning and you might hear the whir of a sewing machine before you hear anyone whisper.

Over the past few years, a growing number of public libraries have added repair tables, tool lending shelves, and volunteer fixers to their weekend schedules. Patrons bring in lamps with frayed cords, jackets with broken zippers, and laptops that refuse to boot, and leave with the item working again or a clear plan for fixing it.

Librarians say the programs fit naturally with their mission. A library has always been a place to borrow things you would rarely use enough to buy, and a soldering iron or a set of precision screwdrivers is not so different from a reference book in that sense.

The volunteers, many of them retired engineers or hobbyists, describe the work as a kind of teaching. They rarely fix the object themselves; instead they guide the owner through the process, so that the next time something breaks, the owner might not need help at all.

Not every repair succeeds. Some devices are glued shut or depend on parts that are no longer made. But organizers estimate that roughly two thirds of the items brought to their tables leave in working order, a rate that has held steady across several seasons.

For many visitors, the appeal is as much social as practical. People linger after their repairs are finished, chatting with the fixers and with each other, and some return the following month as volunteers themselves.
